Salinity

What salinity is, and why it changes

4 minutes

What salinity is

Salinity is the quantity of salt in water. We measure it in practical salinity units. The short form is PSU.

Fresh water has a salinity near 0 PSU. Sea water has a salinity near 35 PSU. The water in a lagoon or an estuary is between the two. We call this water brackish.

How the logger measures salinity

The logger does not taste the water. It measures how well the water carries electricity.

Salt water carries electricity well. Fresh water carries electricity poorly. The logger sends a small current through the water and measures the result. This measurement is the electrical conductivity, or EC.

The logger then calculates salinity from the conductivity. Temperature changes the result, so the logger measures temperature at the same time and corrects for it.

This is important. The logger measures conductivity. It calculates salinity. Many instruments work this way. They measure one quantity and calculate the quantity you asked for.

Why salinity changes

Salinity changes when the quantity of salt changes, or when the quantity of water changes.

The quantity of salt rarely changes quickly. The quantity of water changes often. This gives us two common causes.

Fresh water arrives. Rain falls on the land. The rain flows into creeks and ditches, and the creeks carry it to the lagoon. This fresh water mixes with the salt water and makes it less salty. We call this dilution.

Water evaporates. The sun heats the surface. Water leaves as vapour, but the salt stays behind. The remaining water becomes more salty.

Tides also move salt water in and out. A rising tide usually increases salinity. A falling tide usually decreases it.

The delay

Rain does not change the salinity at once.

The rain must first fall on the land. It must then flow across the ground, into a ditch, into a creek, and out to the water where the logger sits. This journey takes time.

So the salinity falls after the rain, not during it. The size of the delay depends on the distance the water travels and on the shape of the land.

This delay is useful. If you know the delay, you can compare a rainfall record with a salinity record and see whether the two agree.

Why salinity matters

Plants and animals in an estuary live within a range of salinity. Seagrass, oysters, and fish each have a range that suits them.

A slow change gives them time to adjust. A large and sudden change does not. A heavy storm can push the salinity outside the range for a species, and the species can die.

This is one reason we record salinity over time. One measurement tells you the salinity now. A record tells you how quickly it changed, and that is often the more important fact.

Try this

  • Find a fall in salinity in your data. Note the time it started.
  • Look at the rainfall record for the same day. Did rain fall before the fall in salinity?
  • Measure the delay between the two. Write it down.
  • Repeat this for a second event. Is the delay the same?

If the delay is not the same, ask what was different. The quantity of rain, the tide, and the condition of the ground before the rain can all change the result.

Rainfall

Two ways to measure rain, and why they disagree

5 minutes

Two ways to know that it rained

You can measure the rain where you stand. You can also read a record that somebody else made.

Both are useful. Both are correct. They do not always agree, and the reason they disagree is worth understanding.

A rain gauge

A rain gauge is a container with a known opening. Rain falls into the opening and collects in the container. You read the depth on a scale.

The measurement is a depth, usually in millimetres. A reading of 10 mm means the rain would lie 10 mm deep on flat ground.

A rain gauge is accurate, but it measures one place only. It tells you what fell on the gauge. It does not tell you what fell on the next street.

Where you put the gauge changes the result. Keep the gauge away from buildings and trees. A wall or a branch can block the rain, or it can direct extra rain into the container. Record the position of your gauge and do not move it.

A public rainfall record

Weather services publish rainfall records. They build these records from radar, from satellites, and from a network of stations.

To cover a whole country, the service divides the map into cells. It reports one value for each cell. A cell can be several kilometres across.

The value for a cell is an average across the whole cell. This is a sensible way to cover a large area with a limited number of instruments.

Scale is not accuracy

Here is the important idea.

A public record can be accurate and still be wrong for your creek.

Some storms are small. A summer storm can rain hard on one block and leave the next block dry. If that storm covers a small part of a large cell, the average for the cell stays low. The record is a correct average. It is still a poor description of your creek.

Your gauge has the opposite property. It describes one place very well, and it describes nowhere else at all.

What to do when they disagree

Do not decide that one source is lying. Ask what each source can see.

  1. Check the time. The two records must cover the same period.
  2. Check the position. How far is your gauge from the centre of the cell?
  3. Check the size of the storm. A small storm explains a disagreement. A large storm over many hours does not.
  4. Check your gauge. Is it clear of obstructions? Did somebody empty it?

If your gauge shows rain and the public record does not, and the storm was small and local, then you have found the limit of the public record. You have not found an error.

This is a real result. Record it.

Why your measurement counts

Nobody measures the rain on your creek. The nearest official station may be many kilometres away.

When you read your gauge and write the number down, you create information that did not exist before. If you do this often, and if you record where the gauge is, your record becomes useful to other people.

Volunteer rain gauge networks work in exactly this way. Many of the rainfall records that scientists use come from people who read a gauge each morning and wrote the number down.

Try this

  • Read your gauge at the same time each day. Record 0 mm on dry days. A dry day is data.
  • Find the public rainfall record for the same day and the same place. Write both numbers side by side.
  • After two weeks, count the days when the two agree and the days when they do not.
  • Look at the days that disagree. What was different about those days?

Most days will probably agree. The days that do not are the interesting ones.
