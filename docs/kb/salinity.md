## What salinity is

Salinity is the quantity of salt in water. We measure it in practical salinity
units. The short form is PSU.

Fresh water has a salinity near 0 PSU. Sea water has a salinity near 35 PSU.
The water in a lagoon or an estuary is between the two. We call this water
brackish.

## How the logger measures salinity

The logger does not taste the water. It measures how well the water carries
electricity.

Salt water carries electricity well. Fresh water carries electricity poorly.
The logger sends a small current through the water and measures the result.
This measurement is the electrical conductivity, or EC.

The logger then calculates salinity from the conductivity. Temperature changes
the result, so the logger measures temperature at the same time and corrects
for it.

**This is important.** The logger measures conductivity. It calculates
salinity. Many instruments work this way. They measure one quantity and
calculate the quantity you asked for.

## Why salinity changes

Salinity changes when the quantity of salt changes, or when the quantity of
water changes.

The quantity of salt rarely changes quickly. The quantity of water changes
often. This gives us two common causes.

**Fresh water arrives.** Rain falls on the land. The rain flows into creeks and
ditches, and the creeks carry it to the lagoon. This fresh water mixes with the
salt water and makes it less salty. We call this dilution.

**Water evaporates.** The sun heats the surface. Water leaves as vapour, but
the salt stays behind. The remaining water becomes more salty.

Tides also move salt water in and out. A rising tide usually increases
salinity. A falling tide usually decreases it.

## The delay

Rain does not change the salinity at once.

The rain must first fall on the land. It must then flow across the ground, into
a ditch, into a creek, and out to the water where the logger sits. This journey
takes time.

So the salinity falls **after** the rain, not during it. The size of the delay
depends on the distance the water travels and on the shape of the land.

This delay is useful. If you know the delay, you can compare a rainfall record
with a salinity record and see whether the two agree.

## Why salinity matters

Plants and animals in an estuary live within a range of salinity. Seagrass,
oysters, and fish each have a range that suits them.

A slow change gives them time to adjust. A large and sudden change does not.
A heavy storm can push the salinity outside the range for a species, and the
species can die.

This is one reason we record salinity over time. One measurement tells you the
salinity now. A record tells you how quickly it changed, and that is often the
more important fact.

## Try this

- Find a fall in salinity in your data. Note the time it started.
- Look at the rainfall record for the same day. Did rain fall before the fall
  in salinity?
- Measure the delay between the two. Write it down.
- Repeat this for a second event. Is the delay the same?

If the delay is not the same, ask what was different. The quantity of rain, the
tide, and the condition of the ground before the rain can all change the
result.
