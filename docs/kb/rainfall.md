## Two ways to know that it rained

You can measure the rain where you stand. You can also read a record that
somebody else made.

Both are useful. Both are correct. They do not always agree, and the reason
they disagree is worth understanding.

## A rain gauge

A rain gauge is a container with a known opening. Rain falls into the opening
and collects in the container. You read the depth on a scale.

The measurement is a depth, usually in millimetres. A reading of 10 mm means
the rain would lie 10 mm deep on flat ground.

A rain gauge is accurate, but it measures one place only. It tells you what
fell on the gauge. It does not tell you what fell on the next street.

**Where you put the gauge changes the result.** Keep the gauge away from
buildings and trees. A wall or a branch can block the rain, or it can direct
extra rain into the container. Record the position of your gauge and do not
move it.

## A public rainfall record

Weather services publish rainfall records. They build these records from
radar, from satellites, and from a network of stations.

To cover a whole country, the service divides the map into cells. It reports
one value for each cell. A cell can be several kilometres across.

The value for a cell is an average across the whole cell. This is a sensible
way to cover a large area with a limited number of instruments.

## Scale is not accuracy

Here is the important idea.

A public record can be accurate and still be wrong for your creek.

Some storms are small. A summer storm can rain hard on one block and leave the
next block dry. If that storm covers a small part of a large cell, the average
for the cell stays low. The record is a correct average. It is still a poor
description of your creek.

Your gauge has the opposite property. It describes one place very well, and it
describes nowhere else at all.

| | Rain gauge | Public record |
|---|---|---|
| Area | one point | a cell, several kilometres across |
| Tells you | exactly what fell there | the average across the cell |
| Misses | everywhere else | small storms inside the cell |

Neither source is better. They answer different questions.

## What to do when they disagree

Do not decide that one source is lying. Ask what each source can see.

1. Check the time. The two records must cover the same period.
2. Check the position. How far is your gauge from the centre of the cell?
3. Check the size of the storm. A small storm explains a disagreement. A large
   storm over many hours does not.
4. Check your gauge. Is it clear of obstructions? Did somebody empty it?

If your gauge shows rain and the public record does not, and the storm was
small and local, then you have found the limit of the public record. You have
not found an error.

This is a real result. Record it.

## Why your measurement counts

Nobody measures the rain on your creek. The nearest official station may be
many kilometres away.

When you read your gauge and write the number down, you create information
that did not exist before. If you do this often, and if you record where the
gauge is, your record becomes useful to other people.

Volunteer rain gauge networks work in exactly this way. Many of the rainfall
records that scientists use come from people who read a gauge each morning and
wrote the number down.

## Try this

- Read your gauge at the same time each day. Record 0 mm on dry days. A dry day
  is data.
- Find the public rainfall record for the same day and the same place. Write
  both numbers side by side.
- After two weeks, count the days when the two agree and the days when they do
  not.
- Look at the days that disagree. What was different about those days?

Most days will probably agree. The days that do not are the interesting ones.
